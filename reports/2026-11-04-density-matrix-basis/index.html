Mode-space transport · MoS2/HfO2 · how the basis is built

Window density matrix basis

The mode-space basis keeps, for every block of the device, the few directions that the electronic states of an energy window actually occupy. Those directions are the eigenvectors of the density matrix of the window, restricted to the block. This page explains how that density matrix is computed and what the cut on its eigenvalues means.

1. The density matrix of an energy window

Take the energy window W = [μ1, μ2] in which we want transport (here Ec − 0.15 … Ec + 0.6 eV). For a closed system with eigenstates ψn and energies En, the density matrix of the window is the projector onto the states inside it:

ρ = ΣEn ∈ W |ψn⟩⟨ψn| = ∫μ1μ2 A(E) dE,    A(E) = −(1/π) Im G(E + i0)(1)

with the Green's function G(z) = (z S − H − Σ(z))−1. The right-hand form also holds for an open system: with the lead self-energy Σ the states are no longer discrete but form a broadened continuum, and A(E) is its spectral density. We never need ρ of the whole device, only its diagonal block on one piece P of the device, ρPP, built from GPP.

2. Two density matrices, two levels

Level 1 · local density matrix

For one block (a tile or a slab), cut out the block plus every atom within 12 Å of it. Close the outer 3 Å of that embedding with a complex absorbing potential (−iW, ramped up to 0.3 eV), so waves leave instead of reflecting. Where the embedding reaches the contacts, attach the exact lead self-energy there (CAP + OBC). Solve for GPP of this local open problem.

Level 2 · global density matrix

The whole device with the exact lead self-energies ΣL, ΣR. Recursive Green's functions (RGF) give the diagonal blocks Gss of every slab in one sweep. Either on the fine device (direct level 2) or in the level-1 basis, which keeps the level-2 basis inside the level-1 span.

Both use the same contour integral (next section) over the same window. Tile or slab is only the choice of partition; either level can use either one.

3. Integrating on a contour instead of the real axis

On the real axis A(E) is a forest of sharp peaks, one per state, with widths from the lead coupling or the CAP, some of them below 1 meV. Sampling it directly would need thousands of energies. But G(z) is analytic in the upper half plane, so the integral from μ1 to μ2 can be moved onto a semicircle above the axis with the same end points, where G is smooth:

ρPP = −(1/π) Im ∫arc GPP(z) dz ≈ −(1/π) Im Σj=124 wj GPP(zj) z′(θj)(2)

Each point costs one solve for GPP: for level 1 a Schur complement of the embedding (factor the environment, invert the block), for level 2 one RGF sweep. 24 points replace thousands of real energies. The points are independent, so they run in parallel.

The ends of the arc touch the real axis, so states sitting within a broadening of μ1 or μ2 count only partly. Measured on one tile, the 24-point quadrature reproduces ρ to about 1e-4. This is a soft window edge, not a source of missing states: the window is chosen wider than the transport energies.

3b. The contour integral, step by step

This is what the code does (msbasis.rho_contour for level 2, the contour branch of msbasis.tile_modes for level 1). Both levels use the same rule.

  1. Why the path may leave the real axis. With GA = (GR)†, the spectral function is A = (i/2π)(GR − GA). Write X = ∫μ1μ2 GR(E) dE. Then
    ρ = (i/2π)(X − X†) = −(1/π) · (X − X†) / 2i(5)
    the anti-Hermitian part of X. All poles of G(z) = (zS − H − Σ(z))−1 lie below the real axis (pushed there by the leads and the CAP), so G is analytic in the upper half plane. By Cauchy's theorem the integral along the real segment equals the integral along any path above it with the same end points: the semicircle.
  2. Parametrize the arc. Centre c = (μ1 + μ2)/2, radius r = (μ2 − μ1)/2, z(θ) = c + r eiθ, with θ running from π (at μ1) down to 0 (at μ2).
  3. Place the nodes. Take the 24 Gauss–Legendre nodes xj and weights wj on [−1, 1] and map them to [0, π]:
    θj = (π/2)(xj + 1),   w̃j = (π/2) wj,   zj = c + r eiθj,   Δzj = −i r eiθj w̃j(6)
    The minus sign in Δzj comes from running θ from π to 0.
  4. Solve at every node. X ≈ Σj GPP(zj) Δzj, where:
    • Level 1. The block's embedding with A(z) = zS − H + iW − Σemb(z). The CAP W is real and positive, so it also moves poles below the axis. The fast path factors only the environment E of the block: GPP = (APP − APE AEE−1 AEP)−1.
    • Level 2. One RGF sweep over the slabs. Forward: gsL = (Ds − As,s−1 gs−1L As−1,s)−1, with Ds = z Sss − Hss and ΣL, ΣR on the end slabs. Backward: Gss = gsL + gsL As,s+1 Gs+1,s+1 As+1,s gsL.
    • Lead self-energy. Σ(zj) at complex z comes from the folded-lead decimation and is cached on disk per node, so both levels and every rerun share it.
  5. Take the anti-Hermitian part. Level 2 uses eq. (5) as written. In level 1, H, S and W are real, so G is complex symmetric (GT = G), X† is the complex conjugate of X, and eq. (5) reduces to ρ = −Im(X)/π, symmetrized as (ρ + ρT)/2.
  6. Diagonalize and cut (sections 4 and 5).

4. From ρ to a basis: occupations

Diagonalize the block of the density matrix:

ρPP = Σi λi ui ui†,    λ1 ≥ λ2 ≥ … ≥ 0(3)

The eigenvalue λi is the occupation of direction ui: how much of the window's states lives along ui inside the block. Its sum, tr ρPP, counts the window states in the block (83 for slab 1 below; 90–123 per slab for the global density matrix).

  • Large λ (about 1). A state with its energy well inside the window and its weight inside the block.
  • Small λ. The part of a state that lies mostly somewhere else: tails of states centred in a neighbouring block, decay into the oxide, states just outside the window edge, the broadened continuum.

With a non-orthogonal overlap S and the plain (Euclidean) eigenproblem used here, λ is not bounded by 1: the largest are about 3.8. In the S metric (--l2-metric S) they are true occupations in [0, 1]. The ordering, and the meaning of a small λ, are the same.

5. What the cut means

The basis of a block is the set of eigenvectors with λi > cut. The cut says how small a share of the window's states a direction may carry and still be kept. Level 1 uses it as an absolute number; level 2 and the lead CMS use it relative to λmax (about 1, so in practice the same scale).

Each decade of the cut costs 25–40 % more basis vectors and improves the transmission error 5 to 10 times. The small occupations matter because the mode-space solution must also represent how states continue from one block into the next; those continuations are exactly the low-occupation tails. The device has 22 848 orbitals, so even the 1e-10 basis is 16 % of the full size.

Cuts below the ~1e-4 quadrature error of ρ still help: they do not need accurate occupations, only the right directions, which the tails provide.

6. The same idea on the lead: lead CMS

The lead CMS basis is the window density matrix of one periodic lead cell, summed over Bloch states:

ρcell = (1/Nk) Σk ΣEn(k) ∈ W |unk⟩⟨unk|(4)

with the same cut relative to λmax, then copied onto the device atoms that match lead atoms. It describes the ideal lead well but has no coefficients on device atoms without a lead counterpart (in this device the ~880 oxide atoms of the middle slabs). That is the main difference from the level-1 and level-2 density matrices, which are computed on the device itself.
